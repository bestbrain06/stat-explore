Why Does Data Spread Matter?

Two groups can have the same average but tell completely different stories.

Averages describe the centre of data, but they do not reveal how values are distributed. In this lesson, you will discover how mathematicians measure variation using measures of dispersion.

01

What You Will Discover

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Explain the meaning of dispersion in a data set.
  • Calculate range, quartiles and interquartile range.
  • Understand variance as a measure of spread.
  • Calculate and interpret standard deviation.
  • Compare data sets based on their variability.
02

Beyond the Average

The mean is one of the most common ways of describing data. It tells us the central value of a data set. However, two data sets can have the same mean while having very different patterns.

Class A

10, 11, 12, 13, 14

Mean = 12

Class B

4, 8, 12, 16, 20

Mean = 12

Both classes have an average score of 12. But Class B has scores that are much more spread out. This difference is called dispersion.

03

Understanding Dispersion

Dispersion describes how much the values in a data set differ from one another or from the centre of the data. It tells us whether data values are closely grouped together or widely scattered.

Think About It

A classroom where most students score around the same mark has low dispersion. A classroom where scores range from very low to very high has high dispersion.

04

Measures of Dispersion

Mathematicians use different methods to describe how data spreads. The main measures are:

Range
The difference between the largest and smallest values.
Quartiles
Values that divide ordered data into four equal parts.
Interquartile Range
The spread of the middle 50% of the data.
Variance
The average squared distance of values from the mean.
Standard Deviation
The typical distance of values from the mean.
05

Range: Measuring Total Spread

Range is the simplest measure of dispersion. It shows the distance between the highest and lowest value in a data set.

Range = Highest Value − Lowest Value

Worked Example

Find the range of:

5, 8, 12, 15, 20

Step 1: Identify the highest value.
Highest value = 20

Step 2: Identify the lowest value.
Lowest value = 5

Step 3: Subtract.

Range = 20 − 5
Range = 15

Interpretation

The scores in this data set cover a distance of 15 units from the smallest value to the largest value.

06

Quartiles: Understanding the Position of Data

Sometimes the range is affected by very large or very small values. Quartiles give us another way to understand how data is distributed by dividing an ordered data set into four equal parts.

Q1 Q2 (Median) Q3

The three important quartiles are:

  • Q1: The lower quartile. It separates the lowest 25% of the data.
  • Q2: The median. It separates the data into two equal halves.
  • Q3: The upper quartile. It separates the highest 25% of the data.

Worked Example

Find Q1, Q2 and Q3 for:

4, 7, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21

Step 1: Find the median.

4, 7, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21

Q2 = 12

Step 2: Find the lower half.

4, 7, 9

Q1 = 7

Step 3: Find the upper half.

15, 18, 21

Q3 = 18

Why Quartiles Matter

Quartiles help us understand where values are located and whether data is concentrated in certain areas.

07

Interquartile Range (IQR)

The interquartile range measures the spread of the middle 50% of a data set. Unlike range, IQR is not strongly affected by extreme values.

IQR = Q3 − Q1

Worked Example

Given:

Q3 = 25
Q1 = 10

Using:

IQR = Q3 − Q1

IQR = 25 − 10

IQR = 15

Interpretation

The middle 50% of the data values are spread across 15 units.

08

Variance: Measuring Squared Spread

Range and IQR measure spread by looking at distances between values. Variance takes another approach. It measures how far each value is from the mean.

These differences are called deviations. Because positive and negative deviations cancel each other, we square the deviations before finding the average.

Variance = Average of Squared Deviations from the Mean

Worked Example

Find the variance of:

2, 4, 6

Step 1: Find the Mean

Mean = (2 + 4 + 6) ÷ 3

Mean = 4

Step 2: Find Deviations

Value − Mean

2 − 4 = -2
4 − 4 = 0
6 − 4 = 2

Step 3: Square the Deviations

(-2)² = 4
0² = 0
2² = 4

Step 4: Find Average Squared Deviation

Variance = (4 + 0 + 4) ÷ 3

Variance = 8 ÷ 3

Variance = 2.67

Important Idea

A larger variance means the data values are more spread out from the mean. A smaller variance means the values are closer to the mean.

09

Standard Deviation: The Typical Spread of Data

Variance gives us the squared spread of data, but its value is expressed in squared units. Standard deviation solves this problem by taking the square root of the variance. This returns the measurement back to the original units of the data.

Standard Deviation = √Variance

Standard deviation tells us the typical distance between data values and the mean.

Worked Example

Using the variance from the previous example:

2, 4, 6

We found:

Variance = 2.67

Step 1: Take the square root

Standard Deviation = √2.67

Standard Deviation ≈ 1.63

Standard Deviation = 1.63

Interpretation

The standard deviation of 1.63 means that the values in the data set are typically about 1.63 units away from the mean.

10

Comparing Data Using Standard Deviation

Standard deviation allows us to compare how consistent different data sets are. A smaller standard deviation means the values are closer together. A larger standard deviation means the values are more spread out.

Data Set A

48, 49, 50, 51, 52

Small spread

Data Set B

20, 35, 50, 65, 80

Large spread

Although both data sets may have the same mean, Data Set B has greater variation because the values are farther away from the centre.

11

Choosing the Right Measure

Different measures of dispersion provide different information about data.

Range
Useful for quickly finding the total spread.
Quartiles and IQR
Useful when extreme values may affect the data.
Variance
Useful in advanced statistical calculations.
Standard Deviation
The most common measure for describing overall variability.
